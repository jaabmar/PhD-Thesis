\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

When statistical and machine learning models are used in medicine, public policy, or software engineering, predictive accuracy is rarely sufficient on its own. Clinicians need reliable treatment-effect estimates from observational health records, agencies need to publish tabular datasets without revealing personal records, and model providers need language models that do not memorize and reproduce protected text or code.

Each of these three domains carries its own failure mode:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A causal estimate from observational data can be biased by unmeasured confounders that jointly influence treatment assignment and outcomes.
  \item A released tabular dataset can leak private information about the individuals whose records it contains.
  \item A language model can reproduce verbatim or near-verbatim passages of copyrighted material seen during training \citep{henderson2023foundation}.
\end{enumerate}

Formal frameworks exist for all three risks: unconfoundedness and sensitivity analysis in causal inference, differential privacy \citep{dp} for data release, and formal criteria for copyright protection \citep{vyas2023provable}. The core problem studied in this thesis is that these definitions are difficult to apply directly at scale without giving up what makes the data or model useful. In causal inference, testing whether \emph{any} hidden confounding exists rejects even small biases that would not change a decision, while standard sensitivity analysis cannot measure how much confounding is actually present. In private data release, turning noisy differentially private marginals into synthetic tables breaks down on large workloads or ignores the underlying metric geometry of ordered features. In language generation, hard filters or fixed-weight ensembling either degrade fluency and code correctness or leave long memorized spans in the tail.

Across the three main chapters, we design practical tests and algorithms that keep these formal criteria usable in practice: measuring confounding rather than returning a binary flag (Chapter~\ref{chap:confounding-test}), synthesizing large private tables with optimal transport (Chapter~\ref{chap:privpgd}), and curbing verbatim copying at inference time without losing generation quality (Chapter~\ref{chap:cpfuse}).

\label{sec:intro-settings}

  title={Chapter~\ref{chap:confounding-test}: Quantifying Hidden Confounding},
  colbacktitle=chaptercolor!10,
  coltitle=black,
  fonttitle=\bfseries\small
]
\small
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, itemsep=2.5pt, parsep=0pt, topsep=1pt]
  \item[Setting.] Post-marketing surveillance frequently pairs an observational study (such as electronic health records \citep{platt2018fda}) with a randomized trial of the same treatment.
  \item[Question.] Given a randomized trial, what is the minimum strength of hidden confounding present in the observational study?
  \item[Gap.] Standard sensitivity analysis computes how strong confounding would have to be to overturn a conclusion, not how strong it actually is. Binary tests for zero confounding flag minor bias that may not change the decision.
  \item[Approach.] We combine randomized trial estimates with the marginal sensitivity model to test whether hidden confounding exceeds a threshold \(\Gamma \ge 1\). Inverting the tests yields an asymptotically valid lower bound on the true confounding strength.
  \item[Main result.] Valid finite-sample coverage across synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks. On the Women's Health Initiative, the lower bound separates the strongly confounded full cohort from a subgroup with negligible residual bias, whereas a binary test flags both.
\end{description}
\end{tcolorbox}

\small
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, itemsep=2.5pt, parsep=0pt, topsep=1pt]
  \item[Setting.] Publishing synthetic tabular data from differentially private noisy marginals of sensitive records (e.g., census or medical cohorts).
  \item[Question.] Can we generate synthetic datasets directly from noisy marginals while scaling to large workloads and preserving feature geometry?
  \item[Gap.] Graphical-model generators fit discrete cell counts and treat all category errors equally, ignoring distance between ordered values and struggling as workloads expand.
  \item[Approach.] Private Particle Gradient Descent (PrivPGD) embeds the domain, projects noisy marginals onto probability measures, and updates synthetic particles on GPU by minimizing a sliced Wasserstein objective. Differentiable domain-specific constraints enter as penalty terms.
  \item[Main result.] Across nine real datasets with at least \(50{,}000\) records each, PrivPGD ranks first or second on most statistical and downstream metrics, and supports targeted population-level constraints with minimal distortion to unrelated queries.
\end{description}
\end{tcolorbox}

\small
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, itemsep=2.5pt, parsep=0pt, topsep=1pt]
  \item[Setting.] Autoregressive text and code generation from language models fine-tuned on datasets containing copyrighted works.
  \item[Question.] How can we prevent verbatim and near-verbatim reproduction at decoding time while preserving text fluency and code correctness?
  \item[Gap.] Output filters disrupt fluency and break program syntax; fixed-weight fusion (\(\cpdelta\)) still allows one memorized sequence to dominate and leaves long verbatim tails.
  \item[Approach.] CP-Fuse fuses two models trained on data splits whose copyrighted material does not overlap. At every decoding step, it computes history-dependent fusion weights via a local minimax KL optimization, downweighting whichever model assigns higher likelihood to the prefix generated so far.
  \item[Main result.] On overfitted text and code models, CP-Fuse reduces the average length of the longest \(5\%\) of exact matches by more than \(20\times\) relative to the base models and by up to about \(6\times\) relative to token-wise \(\cpdelta\), with pass@1 and fluency close to the unprotected baselines.
\end{description}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Thesis Organization}
\label{sec:intro-org}

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} states the mathematical background used across the three main chapters: potential outcomes and marginal sensitivity analysis, differential privacy and optimal transport, and autoregressive language modeling with KL-based decoding. Chapters~\ref{chap:confounding-test}, \ref{chap:privpgd}, and~\ref{chap:cpfuse} are adapted from three peer-reviewed conference papers \citep{de2023hidden,donhauser2024privacy,abad2025copyright}; full bibliographic details and author contributions are listed in the Publications section in the front matter. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} reviews the main findings, limitations, and open problems. Proofs, implementation details, and extended experimental tables appear in Appendices~\ref{chap:appendix-confounding-test}, \ref{chap:appendix-privpgd}, and~\ref{chap:appendix-cpfuse}.
