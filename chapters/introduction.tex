After a drug is approved, its performance is monitored, increasingly with observational data such as health records \citep{platt2018fda}. At this point, a randomized trial of the same drug may also be available. Current practice either asks how strong hidden confounding would have to be to overturn the observational conclusion, which does not tell how strong it actually is, or tests whether there is any hidden confounding at all, although some confounding may be expected and need not change the conclusion. Chapter~\ref{chap:confounding-test} asks a question in between: given a randomized trial, how much hidden confounding does an observational study contain at least? We compare the treatment effect estimated in the trial with the range of effects that the observational data allow at each confounding strength. Inverting the resulting tests gives a lower bound on the true confounding strength that is valid as the sample size grows. For coronary heart disease in the Women's Health Initiative, comparing this lower bound with the strength needed to overturn the observational conclusion flags the full observational cohort, where strong confounding is present, but not the subgroup in which the main known confounder is removed. A test for any confounding flags both.

Hospitals and public agencies want to share tabular data, such as health or census records, without exposing the individuals in them. A common recipe under differential privacy adds noise to a set of marginals, the joint distributions of small groups of features, and then generates synthetic data that match the noisy marginals. Graphical-model generators fit the cell counts of these marginals, a criterion under which an age that is off by one year counts as much as one that is off by fifty. Chapter~\ref{chap:privpgd} asks whether synthetic data can be built directly from the noisy marginals instead. Private Particle Gradient Descent (PrivPGD) starts from a cloud of synthetic points and moves them by gradient descent so that their marginals approach the noisy ones, measuring the mismatch with a sliced Wasserstein distance that accounts for the distance between values. Constraints enter as additional penalty terms. On nine real datasets with at least \(50{,}000\) records each, PrivPGD ranks first or second in most metrics and datasets, and it can protect selected population-level statistics with little loss on other tasks.

Language models are trained on text and code that can contain copyrighted works, and they can reproduce long passages of it word for word. Filters that block exact matches are less suited to near-verbatim copies and can introduce errors that break the generated code. Chapter~\ref{chap:cpfuse} asks how to prevent such copying at generation time while keeping the text fluent and the code correct. Copyright-Protecting Model Fusion (CP-Fuse) uses two models trained on data whose copyrighted parts do not overlap, so that every protected passage is absent from the training data of at least one of them. At each generation step, CP-Fuse combines the two models and gives less weight to the one that the text generated so far favors, so that neither model dominates the output. On text and code models that were overfitted to memorize their training data, CP-Fuse reduces the average length of the longest \(5\%\) of exact matches by more than \(20\times\) on the three main datasets relative to the overfitted models, and by up to about \(6\times\) relative to the token-wise \(\cpdelta\) method, which combines the models with fixed weights. Code correctness and fluency stay close to those of the overfitted models.

\section{Contributions and Thesis Organization}
\label{sec:intro-org}[THIS PART ALSO SOUDNS TOO LLM GENERATED]

The thesis makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a test of whether hidden confounding in an observational study exceeds a given strength, and an asymptotically valid lower bound on the true confounding strength obtained with randomized trials (Chapter~\ref{chap:confounding-test});
  \item PrivPGD, a method that builds differentially private synthetic tabular data directly from noisy marginals, preserves the geometry of the features, scales to large datasets, and accepts domain-specific constraints (Chapter~\ref{chap:privpgd});
  \item CP-Fuse, an inference-time method that combines two language models with adaptive weights to prevent the reproduction of copyrighted training data while keeping the quality of the generated text and code close to that of the unprotected models (Chapter~\ref{chap:cpfuse}).
\end{itemize}

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} gives a short introduction to observational causal inference and sensitivity analysis, differential privacy and private data release, and language models, memorization, and copyright. Chapters~\ref{chap:confounding-test}, \ref{chap:privpgd}, and~\ref{chap:cpfuse} present the three methods, and Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizes the results, discusses limitations, and outlines future work. Appendices~\ref{chap:appendix-confounding-test}, \ref{chap:appendix-privpgd}, and~\ref{chap:appendix-cpfuse} contain the proofs, experimental details, and additional results for Chapters~\ref{chap:confounding-test}, \ref{chap:privpgd}, and~\ref{chap:cpfuse}. The three main chapters are based on peer-reviewed conference papers \citep{de2023hidden,donhauser2024privacy,abad2025copyright}, and the Publications list gives the full references and contribution notes.
